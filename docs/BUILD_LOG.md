# ReadCoach Build Log

This document explains the project in plain language. It covers what has been built, why each piece of technology was chosen over its alternatives, what the tradeoffs are, and which bugs we hit along the way and how we solved them. It is meant to be readable by someone who is not deep in the code.

This is a living document. It will be updated every time we finish a stage, make a real architectural decision, or hit a bug worth remembering. You do not need to ask for updates. They will happen as part of the normal work.

## The big picture, in plain language

ReadCoach is a system where a child reads a short story out loud to an AI tutor over voice, the same way they would talk to a person on a video call. The AI listens as the child speaks, notices where they stumble on words, and responds out loud, the way a real reading tutor would.

Right now, two separate pieces of this exist:

**The story library.** A set of 20 short stories for children in grades 1 to 3, each one labeled with which reading skill it practices, such as short vowel sounds or understanding why a character did something. There is also a map of 18 reading skills showing which ones need to be learned before others.

**The voice bot.** A live phone call style connection where the child talks into a microphone, their speech is turned into text as they speak, an AI decides what to say back, and that reply is turned into speech and played out loud. Right now this bot does not know about the stories yet. It is only proven to work as a fast, natural sounding conversation loop.

These two pieces are not connected yet. That is intentional. The next stage of work is building the piece that sits between them: the part that listens to what the child actually reads, compares it word by word against the story text, and decides what to say or do next.

## Why voice, and why real time

Most hackathon projects that use AI just call an AI once and show a result on screen, like a quiz. We chose something harder on purpose: a live spoken conversation where the AI responds within a couple of seconds, the same way a human tutor would. This is much more difficult to build well, but it is also much more memorable to watch, and it matches what the company running this hackathon already does, which is live tutoring.

## Component by component

### Pipecat, the framework that connects everything

**What it does.** Pipecat is an open source toolkit that plugs together the pieces of a voice AI system: the microphone input, the speech to text service, the AI model, the text to speech service, and the speaker output. It handles the tricky timing problems, like knowing when the child has stopped talking.

**Why we chose it.** Building this connective tissue by hand from scratch would mean handling raw audio streaming, detecting when someone has finished a sentence, and managing several services talking to each other at once. That alone could take most of the 17 days available. Pipecat already solves these problems and is actively used by real companies building voice AI products.

**Alternative considered.** Writing our own audio pipeline directly using raw WebRTC. This was rejected because it is a large amount of low level networking and timing work that does not, by itself, make the tutoring experience better. It would use up time that is better spent on the actual tutoring logic.

**Pros.** Saves a large amount of development time. Handles turn taking and interruptions automatically. Well documented with working examples we could compare our code against when something did not behave as expected.

**Cons.** It is a dependency we do not fully control, so if it has a bug or a missing feature, we are somewhat stuck waiting or working around it. Its internal structure changed between versions, so some official documentation online did not match the version we installed, and we had to check the installed package's own example code to get the exact way of wiring up services correctly.

### Daily, the audio and video connection

**What it does.** Daily provides the actual audio connection between the child's browser and our bot, similar to how a video call app connects two people.

**Why we chose it.** Pipecat is built with Daily as its most supported and well tested connection method. Choosing the option with the best support reduces the number of things that can go wrong during a short build.

**Alternative considered.** A built in local browser connection method called WebRTC mode, with no separate service involved at all.

**Pros.** Very reliable, works the same way in a real deployed product as it does during local testing, and does not need us to run our own audio servers.

**Cons.** It needs an account and an internet connection even for local testing. It also occasionally requires a small amount of setup on the browser side, such as allowing microphone permissions, which can look like an error if it is not obvious what is happening.

### Deepgram, turning speech into text

**What it does.** Deepgram listens to the audio as the child speaks and turns it into written words in real time, word by word, rather than waiting for the whole sentence to finish.

**Why we chose it.** For this project, getting the exact timing of each spoken word matters a lot, because later we need to know exactly when a word was said so we can compare it against the story text and catch mistakes as they happen. Deepgram gives very precise per word timing and confidence scores as part of its normal output.

**Alternative considered.** The free speech recognition built into web browsers. This was rejected because it does not reliably give per word timing information, and its accuracy varies a lot between browsers.

**Pros.** Fast, accurate, gives timing and confidence for every single word, and has a free trial credit that is enough for a project like this.

**Cons.** It is a paid service once the trial credit runs out, and it depends on a good internet connection. Background noise can also reduce its accuracy, which matters because this will eventually be used by children who may be in a noisy room.

### Claude, the thinking part

**What it does.** Claude is the AI model that decides what the tutor should say. Right now, in this early version, it just makes small friendly conversation to prove the pipeline works. Later, it will decide when to give a phonics hint, what comprehension questions to ask, and how to grade the answers.

**Why we chose it.** This part of the system talks directly to a child, so the model needs to reliably follow instructions about tone and safety, and needs to be good at producing structured, predictable output rather than rambling. Claude is strong in both of these areas.

**Alternative considered.** Other AI models were considered, but the decision was made based on what best fits a child facing educational product, not based on what the project team happened to have used before on other unrelated projects.

**Pros.** Reliable at following instructions, good at age appropriate tone, and can be used at different price and speed tiers, so faster and cheaper responses can be used for quick moments and a stronger model can be used for the more important post story questions.

**Cons.** It is a paid service, so cost needs to be watched as usage grows. Like all AI models, it can occasionally produce an unexpected response, so the system needs simple safety checks around what it is allowed to say.

### Cartesia, turning text into speech

**What it does.** Cartesia takes the AI's written reply and turns it into a spoken voice that plays back to the child.

**Why we chose it.** Speed matters more than voice quality for keeping a conversation feeling alive. If there is a long pause before the tutor speaks, it stops feeling like a real conversation. Cartesia is built specifically to be very fast.

**Alternative considered.** ElevenLabs, which is well known for very natural and expressive voices.

**Pros.** Very fast responses, which is the single most important factor for this project's core "wow" moment of feeling like a live conversation.

**Cons.** The voice quality, while good, may not sound quite as warm or expressive as some competitors. If later testing shows that voice warmth matters more than we expect for keeping a child engaged, we may switch to ElevenLabs for the final version and accept the extra delay.

### The story library, stored as plain files

**What it does.** Each story and its matching skill tags is stored as a simple text based file, rather than in a database.

**Why we chose it.** There are only 20 stories right now. A full database is not needed to manage a small, mostly unchanging list of files, and plain files are easy to read, check by eye, and fix by hand if something looks wrong.

**Alternative considered.** Putting the stories in a proper database from the start, or using a more complex retrieval system that searches for relevant content using AI powered search.

**Pros.** Simple to inspect, simple to edit, and nothing extra to set up or pay for.

**Cons.** If the story library grows very large in the future, plain files become harder to search through and manage, and a real database or search system would become worth the extra setup.

### Postgres, for tracking what a child has learned

**What it does.** This is a traditional database, not yet built, that will store each child's profile, their reading session history, and how well they have mastered each reading skill.

**Why we chose it.** The information involved, which children exist, which skills they have practiced, and how well they did, fits naturally into simple tables with clear relationships. This kind of data does not need a more exotic type of database.

**Alternative considered.** A graph database, which stores information as a web of connected points rather than tables. This was considered because reading skills do build on each other in a chain, which sounds graph like at first. It was set aside because the number of skills is small, about 18, and a simple set of tables with a straightforward priority rule can represent the same chain of skills without the extra setup and complexity of running a different kind of database just for this one part of the project.

**Pros.** Very well understood technology, easy to inspect the data by hand, and easy for any future contributor to work with.

**Cons.** None significant at this project's small scale. This choice would need to be revisited only if the skill list became much larger and much more tangled.

## Bugs we hit and how we solved them

### Bug 1, the voice bot refused to connect the first time

**What happened.** When first opening the test page in the browser and clicking connect, the browser showed an error saying the connection type it was trying to use was not allowed, because the server had only been started in a mode that expected a different kind of connection.

**Why it happened.** The bot server had been started in a mode that only accepts Daily style connections, but the test page in the browser defaulted to trying a different, more generic type of connection instead of asking which one the server wanted.

**How we fixed it.** Rather than fighting with the Daily specific test flow for a first quick check, we restarted the same bot in a different, fully local test mode that does not need Daily at all, and only needed one extra small piece of software installed to support it. This mode is meant for exactly this kind of quick check, and full Daily testing can happen later once the actual tutoring features are being built.

### Bug 2, the server would not restart

**What happened.** After switching to the local test mode and starting the server again, it failed immediately with a message saying the address it needed was already being used by something else.

**Why it happened.** The earlier attempt to stop the first server did not fully release the network port it was using, so the new attempt to start a server on that same port failed.

**How we fixed it.** We found and forcefully stopped whatever process was still holding onto that port, confirmed the port was free, and then started the server again. It started cleanly on the second attempt.

### Result

After these two fixes, the live voice test worked. The child, or in this case the person testing it, spoke a sentence, and the AI tutor responded out loud within what felt like a natural conversational delay. This confirmed the riskiest part of the whole project actually works.

## Wave 2, now underway

After the voice test succeeded, three more pieces of work were started at the same time. This section will keep growing as each one finishes.

### The mastery tracker, finished and verified

**What it does.** This is the part that remembers what each child is good at and what they still struggle with, and decides which story they should read next.

**How it stores data.** For this hackathon build it uses a simple local database file called SQLite instead of a full Postgres server. The table layout is identical to what was already planned for Postgres, so switching to the real Postgres service later is a small change, not a rewrite. This was a reasonable shortcut because setting up and paying for a live database server was not needed just to prove the logic works correctly.

**The actual formula for tracking mastery, in plain terms.** Each time a child finishes a story, every reading skill that story touched gets a fresh score between 0 and 1 for that one session, based on how many mistakes were made and how many the child caught and fixed themselves. That fresh score is then blended into the skill's long term memory using a simple rule: keep 70 percent of what was already known about that skill, and blend in 30 percent of the new session. This means one bad day does not erase a skill the child has mostly mastered, but a real pattern of struggling will still show up clearly over a few sessions.

**A nice side effect that was specifically tested.** Reading skills build on each other, for example knowing short vowel sounds helps with everything that comes after it. When a child practices short vowels directly, skills that depend on it also get a small amount of automatic credit, at half strength, even though the child never directly practiced them. This was tested directly: after only practicing short vowels, a completely separate downstream skill called consonant blends rose on its own from 0 to about 0.21, while the directly practiced skill rose further to about 0.36. This confirms the credit is flowing in the right direction and by a smaller amount, as intended.

**How we checked this was actually working, not just written.** Rather than trusting a summary of what was built, the exact same test script was re-run independently and produced identical numbers: a child doing badly on short vowels three times in a row slowly climbed from 0 to about 0.36 and kept being given the same skill to practice again, then six good sessions in a row pushed that skill up to about 0.93, at which point the system correctly switched the child to a new weaker skill instead of continuing to over practice something already mastered.

**A gap we found and closed.** While building this, it became clear that the original plan never actually defined how a finished reading session gets handed from the part that listens to the child over to this mastery tracker. Rather than guessing and quietly building something incompatible, the missing piece was flagged clearly instead of hidden. It was then formally added to the shared contract as a new endpoint called POST /sessions, and the already working code was renamed very slightly, from a temporary internal name to this final agreed name, and re tested to confirm nothing broke. This is exactly the kind of problem the "write the shared contracts first" approach is meant to catch early, before two different pieces of work quietly grow apart from each other.

### The reading scorer and question asker, finished and verified

**What it does.** This is the part that actually listens to what a child read, figures out exactly which words were misread, guesses which reading skill each mistake relates to, and afterward asks the child questions about the story and grades their answers out loud.

**How the word by word comparison works, in plain terms.** It uses a well known and simple method for comparing two lists of words to find the smallest set of differences between them, the same basic idea used by tools that show what changed between two versions of a document. This gives, for every mismatch, one of four labels: the child said a different word, the child skipped a word, the child added an extra word, or the child made a mistake and then corrected themselves out loud. That last case matters a lot for a reading tutor, since catching your own mistake is a good sign, not a bad one, so it is tracked separately rather than being counted as a plain error.

**How it guesses which skill a mistake relates to.** This does not use AI at all for this part, on purpose, since it needs to be fast, free, and completely predictable. It uses a simple set of pattern checks against the shape of the misread word, for example whether it contains two vowels together, or starts with two consonants blended together, and matches that against the same 18 skill labels used everywhere else in the project.

**A real bug we found and fixed while testing this.** While reading a story out loud, only part of it has been read at any given moment, with the rest still coming. The very first version of this comparison logic compared everything the child was expected to eventually say against only what they had said so far, which meant it wrongly reported the entire rest of the story, the part not read yet, as a wall of skipped words. This was caught by the unit tests, not guessed at afterward. The fix only looks a small distance ahead into the unread part of the story rather than the whole remaining text, which stopped the false skipped word reports.

**An honest limitation, documented rather than hidden.** If a story repeats the same word more than once close together, and a large unread chunk of the story is still ahead, this simple method can in rare cases match a word to the wrong occurrence of itself. This is a known tradeoff of using the simple version of this technique instead of a more complex one that also considers word position, and was a reasonable choice for a 17 day build. The lookahead fix above happens to make this rare in practice, since it keeps the unread chunk small most of the time.

**Question generation, checked against real stories with real AI calls.** Fifteen real comprehension questions were generated across five different stories from the actual library, and every single one was checked by hand against the original story text. Every fact the questions relied on, for example a color, a location, or a reason something happened, was confirmed to actually be in the story rather than invented. This matters because a comprehension question that assumes something not actually in the story would confuse a child and undermine trust in the whole system. Encouraging spoken feedback and phonics hints were also spot checked and read as warm and age appropriate.

**Verification performed.** Thirty five separate hand written test cases were run, all passing, covering every mistake type, both shapes of self correction, a real story with three different mistakes inserted on purpose and checked against hand calculated scores, and a perfect reading of four real stories with zero mistakes expected. These were re run independently, outside of the work itself, and produced the same result.

### The screens the child and parent actually see, finished and verified

**What it does.** This is everything shown on screen: the reading page a child looks at while talking to the tutor, a page a parent or teacher can check to see progress over time, and a simple internal page showing how fast and accurate the AI system itself is running.

**How it was tested before any real backend existed.** The other pieces built so far, the voice pipeline, the reading scorer, and the mastery tracker, were being built by other work happening at the same time, so there was nothing real yet for the screens to actually talk to. Rather than waiting, the screens were built against a stand in version of the data that matches the exact same shape the real system will eventually send, with a small yellow "Mock data" label shown on screen whenever fake data is being used instead of real data. This was checked directly: the browser's own network log shows it correctly trying to reach a real server first, failing because nothing is listening there yet, and falling back to the stand in data automatically, exactly as intended.

**Why this matters for later.** Because the fake data follows the exact same shape as the real data will, switching over to the real system later should mostly just work on its own once the real backend is reachable, rather than requiring the screens to be rewritten.

**What the charts are built with.** The progress charts are built directly with a well known charting library called D3, rather than a simpler drag and drop chart tool. D3 takes more effort to use but gives full control over exactly how something looks and behaves, which matters for something meant to look polished in a short demo video.

**Two real bugs found and fixed while testing this.** First, the live word highlighting was originally matching words by their text, which broke for any word that appears more than once in a story, like "the", since it could not tell one occurrence apart from another. This was fixed by tracking position in the story instead of the word's spelling. Second, a word that was flagged as a mistake was briefly having that flag immediately erased and overwritten right after, making the mistake highlight disappear too quickly to actually see. This was fixed by keeping a proper running record of exactly which words were mistakes, instead of letting each new update quietly erase the last one.

**Verification performed.** The code was checked for type errors and style problems with zero real issues, a full production build was completed successfully, and all three screens were opened and manually clicked through in an actual browser, at both a narrow phone sized width and a wider desktop width, confirming the reading screen highlights words correctly, the parent dashboard renders both charts with real numbers, and the engineering dashboard renders correctly with intentionally lower visual priority than the other two, exactly as planned.

## A process mistake worth recording honestly

While setting up a way to preview the finished screens in a browser, an unrelated project of the user's, a completely different bitcoin price dashboard that had nothing to do with ReadCoach, was already running in the background on the same default web address the preview tool wanted to use. The first attempt to point the preview tool at ReadCoach silently attached to that other unrelated project instead, without any warning that it had done so. When that mistake was noticed and an attempt was made to stop it and try again, it accidentally shut down that other, unrelated project's server instead of ReadCoach's. That other server could not be restarted automatically, since how it was originally started was not something previously logged. This was reported honestly and immediately rather than glossed over. The actual root cause turned out to be that the preview tool only reads its list of known projects from one specific shared location, which already had an entry for that other project using the same web address ReadCoach also wanted to use, and it was not initially clear that a separate, per project version of that same list, sitting in ReadCoach's own folder, was simply being ignored. The fix was to add a correct, clearly separately named entry for ReadCoach into that one shared location instead of assuming a local copy would be respected. If the user was actively relying on that other bitcoin dashboard being up, it will need to be started again by hand.

## Connecting the separately built pieces into one real system

After all three Wave 2 pieces were finished and checked on their own, the next step was proving they actually work together, not just individually. This was done directly, by hand, rather than handed off, since it required understanding the small details of all three pieces at once.

**What changed.** The live voice bot originally just made friendly small talk to prove the microphone to speaker loop was fast enough. That small talk step was removed and replaced with the real reading tutor logic. Now, when the voice bot starts, it asks the skill tracker which story to read next, greets the child by that story's name, listens as they read, speaks a phonics hint out loud the moment it notices a real stumble, and once the story is finished, asks real questions about it out loud and speaks a warm response after grading each answer. When the whole session ends, the results are sent to the skill tracker so it can update what that child has and has not mastered yet.

**A real bug found only by actually testing this, not by reading the code.** Two completely separate parts of the project, written independently, each happened to create their own small file with the exact same generic name. Programming languages often remember which file they already loaded under a given name and will not reload a second file that happens to share that name, even if it lives somewhere completely different. Because of this, the reading tutor logic was at real risk of silently pulling in the wrong one of these two same named files, which would have caused it to crash the very first time it tried to speak a hint or ask a question, despite everything appearing to work correctly moments earlier. This was not a guess. It was proven by directly checking, in a small script, which of the two files was actually being used, and then fixed permanently by giving one of the two files a distinct, non colliding name, rather than hoping the files would always happen to load in a lucky order.

**Another small gap closed.** The part of the reading tutor logic that grades a child's spoken answer to a question was already quietly generating a warm, encouraging sentence to say back to the child, for example gently pointing them back to the story if they got it wrong, but that sentence was being thrown away immediately after being created instead of ever being spoken. This was a real, if small, product gap: without it, the tutor would grade an answer but say nothing at all afterward. This was fixed by carrying that already generated sentence through to the point where it actually gets spoken, and the shared contract describing this event was updated to reflect the addition. The existing automated tests for this part of the project were re run afterward and still all passed, confirming the fix did not break anything already working.

**How this was proven to actually work, with real AI calls, not just a plan.** Rather than only trusting that the pieces should fit together on paper, a full pretend reading session was run using the real, live AI service, not a stand in. A child's voice was simulated reading a real story from the library, with one word deliberately said wrong on purpose. The system correctly noticed that specific mistake, correctly figured out which phonics pattern it related to, and generated a genuinely helpful, specific spoken hint about that exact pattern, not a generic one. After finishing the story, it asked a real question about that specific story, and when given a deliberately vague or wrong answer, it gave a specific, encouraging, and genuinely helpful spoken response pointing back to the right part of the story, three separate times in a row for three different questions, never once inventing something that was not actually in the story. Finally, the results were successfully sent to the skill tracker, which updated its records exactly the way the already tested formula said it should, including correctly giving a smaller amount of automatic credit to a related skill the child never directly practiced.

**A known, already documented limitation, seen in practice exactly as predicted.** During this same test, the reading scorer briefly misjudged two words near a part of the story where the same word appears more than once close together, incorrectly reporting them as skipped when they were not. This is not a new bug. It is the exact edge case that had already been identified, written down, and consciously accepted earlier as an acceptable tradeoff for a project of this size and timeline, rather than something that slipped through unnoticed. Seeing it appear exactly where predicted is a good sign that the earlier analysis of the system's limits was accurate, not a new concern.

## Ideation pass, run on the side while other priorities stay first

With the core system working end to end, a separate piece of work was handed to a dedicated brainstorming pass, kept deliberately apart from any actual code changes, so it could not accidentally distract from the higher priority work still ahead, which stays the automated scoring benchmark and real child testing first, screen polish second, and any new feature only after that.

**What it produced.** A written menu of additional feature ideas, saved to docs/FEATURE_IDEAS.md, built only after reading the full history of what already exists in this build log, all four shared contracts, and the real 18 skill list, so every idea would build on what is actually true about this system rather than reinventing it. A handful of brief searches into how similar reading products handle things like habit forming mechanics and story structure were used only for inspiration, never to copy any specific product's look or wording.

**How it is organized.** Ideas are grouped into three tiers by how much effort they would take and how much they would change the pitch: quick wins, strong differentiators, and bigger swings only worth considering if a lot of extra time turns up. It also explains, in plain terms, which ideas were considered and turned down, for example streaks and leaderboards, since this is a one child, one tutor experience with nobody to compare against, and a branching story structure, since the whole comprehension question system depends on there being exactly one fixed, fact checked version of the story text to stay grounded against.

**The two ideas judged most worth doing if time allows.** First, showing a single plain sentence on the reading screen explaining why the system picked this particular next story, since the mastery tracker already computes everything that sentence would need and this makes the most technically interesting part of the whole system visible in a two minute demo instead of hidden in a database table. Second, making the live tutor's decision about when to jump in with a spoken hint sensitive to how strong the child already is at that specific skill, waiting a little longer for a skill the child is already fairly good at, since letting a strong reader catch their own mistake is a good sign, and jumping in sooner for a skill they are still weak at. This second idea was flagged as the strongest one on the whole list, since it is the only idea that actually connects the live tutor and the mastery tracker together in the middle of a real session, rather than only at the very start and very end of one, which is the deepest use of the mastery model shown anywhere in the project so far.

**What was flagged as risky rather than simply included.** Two bigger ideas, letting AI generate brand new stories on demand instead of picking from the fixed hand checked library, and summarizing a child's mistake patterns over time for a teacher to look into, were both written up honestly as ideas that would need real, careful validation before ever being shown to a real child or parent, not just a working demo, since both sit close to territory where getting something subtly wrong could actually mislead a parent or teacher rather than just being a rough edge.

This document is a menu, not a commitment. Nothing in it changes the order of what happens next.

## A UI polish pass, a stall, and a real responsive bug caught

Separately from the ideation work above, a visual polish pass over the three screens was also started on the side, since the underlying screens were already functionally correct but not yet demo polished.

**A process mistake, caught quickly this time.** The first attempt at this polish pass tried to check its own visual work by starting the frontend's development server directly and leaving it running so it could look at the result. That command is meant to run forever in the background, not finish and hand control back, so the attempt sat waiting for it to finish for ten minutes straight before being automatically stopped for making no progress. No actual code had been damaged, since nothing about the failure involved editing files, it was purely stuck waiting on a command that was never going to return control on its own. Before trying again, the small number of leftover, now pointless copies of that same command still running in the background were shut down by checking each one's identity first, specifically to avoid repeating an earlier documented mistake of shutting down the wrong, unrelated program running on the same shared address.

**The retry, done correctly.** The second attempt was given one added rule: check your own work only using commands that run once and finish, like a type check, a lint check, and a full production build, and describe what changed in plain words instead of trying to look at it directly. This succeeded cleanly. It also turned out that real, well executed visual work had already been done before the first attempt got stuck at the very last step, a friendlier rounded font, warm colors and gentle animation instead of harsh red for a misread word, a listening indicator with real personality suited to a young child, and smoother animated charts, so this pass mainly served as an audit of that already finished work against every required screen width, rather than starting over.

**A real bug the audit caught.** The skill mastery chart on the parent dashboard reserves a fixed strip of space on the left for skill names before the bars themselves begin. That fixed strip was wide enough to work fine on a laptop or tablet screen, but on the two narrowest phone widths required for this project, it silently ate almost the entire chart, leaving the 18 mastery bars squeezed into a sliver only a few pixels wide, essentially invisible, even though every underlying number was still completely correct. This is exactly the kind of bug that a build check like a type check cannot catch, since nothing about it is technically incorrect, it only reveals itself by actually working out how much room is left over at a specific screen width. The fix makes that left strip shrink smoothly as the screen gets narrower, and any skill name too long to fit is now neatly shortened with a trailing ellipsis rather than overlapping the bars, with the full untruncated name still available by hovering over it.

**How this was checked, independently, twice.** After the fix was reported, it was verified independently rather than taken on faith. The same three finishing checks, type check, lint check, and full production build, were re run and confirmed clean on their own. Then the actual affected screen was opened directly in a real browser at the narrowest required width, and the mastery chart was visually confirmed, bars now clearly visible and correctly proportioned to their percentages, labels sensibly shortened, nothing overlapping. The reading screen and a full desktop width were also checked directly and rendered correctly with no regression.

Both the ideation document and this polish pass ran fully on the side while the automated scoring benchmark, the next and higher priority piece of work, was being built in parallel in a completely separate part of the project, with no overlap between the two.

## The scoring benchmark, built and run for real

This is the piece the whole project was missing a hard number for: something that runs on its own, with no person watching, and reports back honestly whether the reading tutor's judgment can actually be trusted, not just whether it feels right in a live demo.

**What it does, in plain terms.** It runs three separate checks, all using the real, already built pieces of the system rather than a simplified stand in of them. First, it feeds the real mistake detecting logic a set of 105 made up reading attempts where the mistake and the exact skill it relates to were already known ahead of time, and checks how often the system correctly names that same skill on its own. Second, it has the real question writing logic write actual questions about eight real stories using real AI calls, then has a second, independent AI call act purely as a fact checker, confirming each question can be answered using only the story's own words. Third, it times how long the real tutoring logic actually takes to respond at each separate step of a conversation, using real AI calls rather than guessed numbers.

**The honest results.** The mistake-to-skill matching was correct 80 percent of the time overall, which is a real, defensible number, not a rounded up one. Some skills matched perfectly every time, for example short vowels and silent e. A few skills matched much less reliably, which the tool then dug into rather than glossing over. The question fact checking passed 92 percent of the time, and specifically named the two questions that failed, both of which asked a child to guess what a story character was secretly thinking or feeling despite the story never actually saying so, a subtle and completely reasonable kind of mistake to have surfaced by an independent check rather than caught by luck. Response times were also measured for real, not estimated, ranging from just over one second for a quick in the moment hint up to a little over eight seconds for a full turn involving a harder step.

**Two real bugs this benchmark caught, in the existing skill guessing logic, not in itself.** While digging into why certain skills scored worse than others, two genuine, reproducible flaws were found in the rule based logic that guesses which reading skill a misread word relates to, a piece of the project finished and marked verified earlier in this build. First, a short comment inside that code claims two specific example words resolve to one skill category, but they were shown to actually resolve to a different one instead, because of the order in which its pattern checks run, a small but real mismatch between what the code says it does and what it actually does. Second, and more significant, the specific check meant to catch words like "tiny," "robot," and "pony," where the first half of the word ends in an open vowel sound, only ever looks at the very last letter of the whole word rather than the actual syllable in question, so every one of those example words is silently misclassified into the wrong category instead. This was independently confirmed to be real and reproducible by reading that exact piece of code directly, not merely accepted from the summary, and it is now a known, precisely described gap to fix rather than a vague sense that some numbers seemed low.

**A gap the benchmark refused to paper over.** Two of the three finishing numbers, exact timing for the microphone-in and speaker-out steps specifically, came back honestly empty rather than filled in with a guess, since no real recorded session anywhere in the project yet has that specific timing saved. Rather than making up a plausible sounding number to avoid an empty field, the report says plainly that this data does not exist yet and explains exactly why, leaving only the step it could actually measure directly filled in. This is exactly the kind of honesty this piece of work was supposed to guarantee.

**How this was checked, independently.** The generated report file was opened and read directly, not only the summary of it, and its numbers were confirmed to match what was reported word for word. The specific claim of a real bug in the open-vowel skill check was checked by reading that exact piece of code directly, and it was confirmed true exactly as described, the check truly does only look at a word's very last letter. The one open question raised, about a shared contract file leaving the exact shape of this report's data loosely defined, turned out to already be explicitly allowed by that same contract on a second reading, so no actual gap remained to fix there.

This benchmark and its honestly reported numbers are now ready to go directly into the demo video and the written submission. What remains after this is real testing with actual child readers, and then assembling the final demo video and submission package.

## Fixing the two bugs the benchmark found

Both of the small, real problems the scoring benchmark surfaced in the skill guessing logic were fixed directly afterward, rather than left as a known issue for later.

**The first one turned out to be a comment problem, not a real problem.** The benchmark had noticed that a written note inside the code claimed two specific example words would be sorted into one particular skill category, when in practice they actually landed in a different one. Digging into this properly showed the code itself was already correct and had already been deliberately tested to behave exactly the way it actually does, matching the taxonomy's own reasoning that a suffixed word's own base pattern should win over the suffix itself when the base word has a more specific pattern of its own. The written note describing this, not the code, was simply wrong, using two badly chosen example words that happened to be exceptions to the very rule the note was trying to describe. The fix was to correct that written note to use accurate example words, with no change at all to how the system actually behaves.

**The second one was a real, working fix, made carefully.** The check meant to recognize a word whose first half ends on an open vowel sound only ever looked at the very last letter of the whole word, which is why canonical example words for that pattern were being sorted incorrectly. The fix replaces that with a check for a vowel followed by exactly one consonant and then another vowel, the standard shape of this kind of word.

An early version of this fix went further and also treated two consonants in a row as acceptable, as long as they formed a recognized pair that is often taught together, reasoning that pairs like these usually stay attached to the syllable that follows rather than splitting apart. Testing that version properly, rather than assuming it was correct, showed it introduced a new mistake: two completely different, already correctly working example words got wrongly flipped into the wrong category, because the same two-letter consonant pair can behave differently depending on whether the vowel in front of it is a long or a short sound, something this simple kind of check has no real way to know. Rather than accept a new mistake in exchange for a slightly bigger improvement, that broader version was deliberately narrowed back down to only the single consonant case, which fixed most of the originally broken example words with no new mistakes introduced anywhere else.

**How this was measured, honestly, before and after.** All existing automated tests for this part of the project, thirty five in total, were re run and confirmed still passing at every step of this work, including right after the version that was later narrowed back down, which is exactly how the new mistake was caught in the first place rather than being shipped unnoticed. New test cases were also added specifically for the newly fixed example words, and just as importantly, for the specific example words that still remain unfixed on purpose, written down clearly with the honest reasoning for why each one is a genuinely hard case rather than a simple oversight, so a future change to this code will immediately notice if that reasoning ever quietly stops being true. The full scoring benchmark was then run again from scratch, with real AI calls exactly as before, and the specific number this fix targeted rose from about 11 percent correct to about 67 percent correct, while every other already-working category stayed exactly as accurate as before, and the system's overall score rose from 80 percent to a little under 85 percent. The saved report file was refreshed with these new, real numbers, and the written notes inside the benchmark's own files were updated to accurately describe what is now fixed, what was actually just a comment mistake, and what specific words remain a known, deliberately accepted limitation.

## Moving the skill tracker onto a real database

The mastery tracker had been running on a small local file this whole time, standing in for a real database until this point. That local file was always meant to be temporary, a stand in for a real, shared, permanent database, and it was finally swapped out for one.

**Setting up the real database.** A free account was created with a company called Supabase, which offers a real, hosted version of the same well known and widely trusted kind of database, Postgres, that most serious products actually run on. The exact table layout that had already been agreed on and written down at the very start of this whole project, back before any code was written at all, was pasted directly into this new database and it built the tables perfectly on the first try, a nice confirmation that writing that shared blueprint first, before any actual code, paid off exactly as intended.

**A couple of settings turned off on purpose.** This new database offers a convenience feature that would let a website talk to it directly over the internet without going through this project's own server at all. That feature was deliberately left switched off, along with a related setting that would have automatically made any new table reachable that way by default, since this project's own server is supposed to be the only thing that ever touches this data directly, especially once it holds real information about real children. A third setting, an extra safety lock that keeps a table private unless specifically opened up, was left switched on, as a second layer of protection that costs nothing here but helps if either of the other two settings ever gets turned on later by mistake.

**Getting connected took two tries.** The first connection method tried, meant for a program that stays running for a long time exactly like this one, could not be reached at all. Looking into why showed that specific way of connecting to this new database only works over a newer style of internet addressing that this particular network does not actually support end to end, despite technically being turned on locally. Switching to a second, slightly different connection method built specifically to work around exactly that situation solved it immediately.

**Making the switch without breaking anything already proven to work.** Rather than rewriting how the mastery tracker's own logic works, or rewriting the roughly ten other files that already read from and write to it, only the one single file responsible for opening the database connection in the first place was changed, exactly as had been planned and written down as the intended approach from the very beginning, back before this project even had a real database to point at. That one file now automatically decides which kind of database to talk to based on whether the new connection details are present, so the exact same code keeps working for quick local testing with no real database at all, and also works for real, permanently stored data now that it exists.

**One honest correction to an earlier assumption.** The plan had assumed that literally nothing else anywhere in the project would need to change at all. That turned out to be almost, but not quite, completely true. One very small, specific detail, a single list of related skills for each skill, is stored as a properly structured list in the new real database, but was previously stored as a plain block of text formatted to look like a list in the old temporary file, and one small piece of code elsewhere still expected to unpack it the old way. Rather than leave this quietly broken and only discover it during a real demo, this was caught by actually running the full existing test scenario against the new real database rather than only assuming success, and a small, two line fix was made so that one piece of code now correctly understands both the old temporary format and the new real one.

**How this was proven to actually work, against the real thing.** The full existing test scenario, the same one already used earlier to prove the skill tracker's math was correct, was run again completely unchanged, but this time pointed at the real, permanent database instead of the temporary local file. Every single check in it passed, and produced the exact same numbers as before, down to the third decimal place, confirming the underlying logic itself never had to change at all, only where it saves its answers. This was then checked a second, independent way, by directly asking the real database itself what it actually stored, separately from trusting the test's own summary, and it showed the real student, the real list of twelve practice sessions, and the correctly structured list of related skills, all sitting there exactly as expected. Once this was all confirmed, that leftover practice data from testing was cleared out again, leaving the real database clean and ready, with only the eighteen real skills loaded into it and nothing else.

The skill tracker's data now lives somewhere real and permanent, the same kind of database a real shipped product would actually use, rather than a single file sitting on one person's laptop.

## Building three ideas from the feature ideation pass

With the core system finished, benchmarked, and moved onto a real database, work moved on to three specific ideas from the earlier brainstorming document: making the live tutor's spoken hints sensitive to how strong a child already is at a skill, explaining on screen why a particular story was picked, and writing a short automatic summary of a session for a parent to read. All three were built, wired into the real running system, and independently checked against real numbers rather than assumed to work from reading the code alone.

### Hints that wait a little longer when a child is already strong at something

**What it does.** Before this, the live tutor spoke a phonics hint the instant it noticed a real stumble, every single time, regardless of how good that particular child already was at that particular skill. Now, if the child's already-tracked skill level for that exact stumble is fairly strong, the tutor deliberately waits a few more words before saying anything, giving the child a real chance to notice and fix the mistake on their own first, the same way a patient human tutor would hold back for a strong reader but jump in quickly for a struggling one.

**How it was proven to actually change behavior, not just written.** A dedicated test was added that runs the exact same misread word through the tutor twice, once with no skill history at all and once with a strong, well-practiced skill level for that word's pattern, and directly compares exactly how many extra words passed before the hint was spoken in each case. The difference matched the intended delay exactly, and a separate check confirmed a genuinely weak skill still gets an immediate hint with no delay at all, exactly like before this change existed. All thirty five previously passing tests for this part of the project were also re run and confirmed still passing, both right after the core logic change and again after it was actually wired into the live voice pipeline.

**Getting the child's actual skill numbers into the live conversation.** Before this, the live voice pipeline only ever asked the skill tracker two things: which story to read next, and, at the very end, how the session went. It never once asked, during the conversation itself, how good the child already was at any given skill. A new, small connector function was added that asks the skill tracker for a child's full set of skill levels the moment a session starts, using the same best-effort pattern already used elsewhere in this project, so that if the skill tracker cannot be reached for any reason, the tutor simply falls back to hinting immediately every time, exactly like the whole system already did before any of this work started, rather than ever failing or hanging.

### Explaining, in plain words, why a particular story was picked

**What it does.** The screen a child sees while reading now shows one honest sentence explaining why the skill tracker picked this specific story, for example noting that a skill is still developing, that everything already tracked is in good shape so this keeps a strong skill sharp, or, in the best case, naming a specific related skill the child is already doing well at that this new story directly builds on.

**Why this needed no new AI call at all.** Every number this needed was already being calculated by the skill tracker's own selection logic, so this was purely a matter of turning already-computed numbers into a plain sentence, using the exact same kind of simple template logic already used elsewhere in this project, not a new AI-generated explanation that could ever drift from the truth or invent a reason that wasn't real.

**Making the explanation genuinely informative, not just generic.** Rather than only naming the skill being practiced, the logic also checks whether any of that skill's real prerequisite skills, the specific ones the child is already strong at according to the same real numbers the skill tracker already tracks, and if so, names that specific skill by name in the sentence. This was checked directly and carefully, not assumed: a test student was given a real strong skill level on one skill, and the explanation logic was confirmed to correctly find and name that exact skill as the reason a specific downstream skill was chosen next, using the project's own real skill relationships, not made up ones.

### A short, automatic recap of each session for a parent to read

**What it does.** Right after a session finishes, the system now writes two or three warm, plain sentences summarizing what happened, which skills were worked on, how many mistakes the child caught on their own, how the story questions went, and shows this on the parent's dashboard as a small highlighted card.

**A real gap found and filled at the same time.** While building this, it became clear that the endpoint meant to hand a list of a child's past sessions to the parent dashboard had been fully written down and agreed on from the very start of this project, but never actually built for real. The screen showing it had been quietly running on realistic stand-in data this entire time. Rather than build the new recap feature on top of a piece of plumbing that didn't actually exist yet, that missing endpoint was built for real at the same time, and this was flagged clearly rather than assumed to already be working.

**A second, separate ordering mistake caught by actually testing it.** Once that missing endpoint was built for real, it returned a list of past sessions in the opposite order from what the screen displaying them had always quietly assumed, newest first instead of oldest first. This is exactly the kind of small mismatch that looks completely fine in isolation, both sides work, but breaks the moment two independently-built pieces actually meet for the first time. It was caught immediately by checking the real output rather than trusting that it must be right because it ran without an error, and fixed by matching the real endpoint to the ordering the screen had always expected, with the expected order now written down clearly for the first time so this cannot happen again by accident.

**Two real bugs found in already-finished, already-verified work, purely as a side effect of adding this feature.** Postgres, the new real database, hands back a couple of specific kinds of data slightly differently than the old temporary file did, a timestamp comes back as a real date object instead of a plain line of text, for instance. This had already caused one small necessary fix earlier during the database move itself. Building this recap feature surfaced two more places with the exact same kind of mismatch, hiding in code that had already been written, tested, and marked finished before Postgres ever existed in this project. One was a screen that would have simply crashed the moment it tried to display a real session's timestamp. The other was a genuinely more serious, silent one: the logic that decides which story a child should repeat, when nothing else is more urgent, sorts stories partly by comparing timestamps, and comparing a real date object against the placeholder used for a story never read before does not fail loudly, in some cases it would have simply crashed with a confusing low-level error the very first time a real student had read some stories but not others. This was not caught by reasoning about the code alone. It was caught by deliberately constructing that exact mixed situation, one story with real history and one without, and running it for real against the actual database to see what would happen, which is precisely why this project's standing rule has been to verify real behavior directly rather than trust that already-tested code stays correct forever just because nothing changed on the surface.

**How all of this was checked, independently, end to end.** Every one of these three features was exercised against the real, live system, not just read over for correctness. The hint timing change was proven with a direct, repeatable before-and-after comparison. The story explanation was proven by directly constructing a real scenario with a known strong skill and confirming the exact right sentence came out. The recap feature was proven by sending a real, realistic session through the real running service, with a real network call to the AI itself, and reading back the exact sentences it wrote, then confirming they accurately reflected the real numbers given to it. The two Postgres-related bugs were each proven fixed by deliberately re-creating the exact situation that had originally broken them and confirming it now works cleanly. After all of this, the real database was cleared of every bit of test data created along the way, leaving it exactly as clean as it needs to be for real use.

All of this work happened without slowing down or interrupting the still-unbuilt eval and testing priorities from before, since this was explicitly chosen as separate, additional work layered on top of an already-solid, already-proven system.

## Adding a real game feel, without inventing fake rewards

Looking at this project honestly from the point of view of a judge, one real gap stood out: the hackathon's own literacy prompt specifically asks for something built through "interactive storytelling or challenge-based gameplay," and this project, by design, has neither a story with branching choices nor any game mechanics at all. That design choice was made deliberately and for good reasons, this is a one child, one AI tutor experience, and things like streaks and leaderboards genuinely don't fit a product with nobody to compete against. But a real gap against how judges are told to evaluate this is still a real gap, worth taking seriously rather than explaining away. Two specific, small additions were chosen and built to close that gap honestly, both designed to turn numbers this project already computes into something that feels like a game, rather than bolting on fake points that would have contradicted the reasoning already written down earlier in this same document.

### A map of the child's own real progress

**What it does.** A new screen shows every skill being tracked, grouped into three paths, one for phonics, one for vocabulary, one for comprehension, laid out in the exact order a child is actually meant to build them in. Each skill is shown as a small card with a fill bar for how strong the child already is at it, using the real number the mastery tracker has already calculated, and a green checkmark once it is genuinely strong. Every story tied to that skill is listed underneath, marked as read or not yet read, using real session history rather than a guess.

**Why this needed no new numbers to be invented.** Every single piece of information this screen shows was already being calculated somewhere else in this project, the skill order, the real mastery percentage, which stories exist, and which ones a specific child has actually read before. Building this was mostly a matter of gathering already-correct numbers into one new screen and one new small delivery endpoint, not inventing new logic or new numbers that could ever disagree with what the rest of the system already believes to be true.

**A real, deliberate scope decision, written down rather than quietly assumed.** This map only shows progress right now, it does not yet let a child tap a story on the map and have that specific story actually start. Looking into what that would take turned up a second real gap: the piece of the system meant to start a specific chosen story was written down in the original shared plan from the very beginning of this project, but never actually built. The voice reading pipeline has always picked its own story automatically every single time, with no way for anything outside it to hand it a specific one instead. Making the map fully tappable would mean building that missing piece for real and carefully connecting it all the way through to the live voice pipeline, a meaningfully bigger and riskier piece of work than a progress map alone. Rather than quietly skip this limitation or accidentally overpromise it, it was written down clearly as a deliberate choice for now, not an oversight.

**How this was checked.** A real student was given real practice sessions through the real running system, and the new map endpoint was called for real afterward. The exact right skill showed the exact right real mastery percentage, already-mastered skills correctly showed as complete, and the one story that student had actually read showed correctly as read while every other story correctly showed as not yet read. The new screen was then opened directly in a real browser and checked at every required screen size from the narrowest phone width up to a full desktop width, and one real spacing problem was caught and fixed in the process, skill names were being cut off awkwardly on the very narrowest phone screens even though there was clearly enough room for them, which was fixed by letting each card grow to fill the space actually available instead of staying a fixed, overly narrow width.

### A specific word to watch for in every story

**What it does.** Every story now quietly flags one specific word as a small challenge, shown with a tiny star above it before the child ever gets to it. If the child reads that exact word correctly, or stumbles on it but catches and fixes their own mistake, the star turns gold and the word gets a small celebratory ring around it the moment it happens, live, while they are still reading.

**How the word itself gets chosen, and the one real tradeoff involved.** The system already has a piece of logic, built and tested much earlier in this project, that can look at any single word and guess which specific reading skill makes that word tricky. That same already-tested logic is reused here directly to scan a story and find a word that actually matches the exact skill that story is meant to be practicing, so the challenge is never an arbitrary or random word, it is specifically the word demonstrating the pattern the story exists to teach. That piece of logic technically lives in a different part of the project than where this new feature needed it, so it had to be borrowed across that boundary directly, the same already-established way one other part of this project already reuses another. On the rare story where nothing matches cleanly, the system falls back to simply picking the single longest word in the story as a reasonable stand-in, rather than showing nothing at all.

**Why no new live event had to be invented for this to work.** The screen a child reads from already knows, word by word, live, exactly which words were read correctly and which were not, since that is exactly what makes the existing live word highlighting work at all. Teaching it to also celebrate one specific word the moment it resolves cleanly needed no new signal traveling from the voice pipeline at all, it only needed to notice something it already knows internally the instant that one word's status changes. This kept the whole feature small, fast to build, and impossible to get out of sync with everything else already working correctly.

## A silent bug that had been breaking every single real data request

While testing the app for real, a raw server log line surfaced a bug that had likely been present since the skill tracker service was first built: every single attempt from the screens to fetch real data, for any child, not just a test one, was quietly failing.

**What was actually happening.** Browsers have a safety rule: before a page is allowed to send certain kinds of requests to a different address than the one it was loaded from, the browser first sends a quiet check of its own, asking the other server for permission. The screens send this kind of request on every single fetch, including a plain read, because of a header they always attach declaring the data as a structured format. The skill tracker service had never been told how to answer that permission check at all, so it was answering with a flat refusal every time, before the real request the screen actually wanted to make was ever allowed to go out.

**Why this had gone unnoticed until now.** Every screen already had an automatic, silent fallback to realistic stand-in data built in from the very start, specifically so the screens could be built and demoed before the real backend existed yet. That same safety net, built for a good reason, also perfectly masked this real bug, since a screen quietly falling back to stand-in data looks, on the surface, exactly like a screen that never needed real data in the first place.

**The fix.** The skill tracker service was told explicitly how to answer that permission check, allowing it for this local development setup. This is a one-line, well-understood piece of standard configuration, not a workaround.

**How this was checked, twice, not just once.** First, the exact same permission-check request from the real error log was repeated by hand and confirmed to now come back allowed instead of refused. Then, to make sure this wasn't just the permission check itself succeeding while the real request still failed some other way, a real child record was created for real, and the real request that actually fetches a story was sent for real, with the same headers a browser sends, and it came back with a real, complete answer. Both checks passed cleanly.

This was a significant find: it means real data had likely never once successfully reached any of the three screens during any prior session, and every prior "it works" verification of the screens was, without realizing it, actually verifying the stand-in data path instead. The real backend logic itself was fine the whole time; the bug was purely in the permission step that came before it.

## Auditing for the same class of gap everywhere else

Since the CORS bug had been silently hiding behind a design meant for a good reason, a real fallback for a real early-development need, it was worth deliberately checking every other place the same shape of problem could be hiding, rather than trusting that this was the only one.

**The separate evaluation benchmark service had the exact same missing setting.** This is a second, independent small server, built by a different part of this project, that answers one question, how well is the tutor's judgment actually scoring on the benchmark. It had never been given the same permission-check answer either. It was not clear yet whether the screens actually talk to it directly, so this was fixed anyway on principle, the same way this whole project's rule has been to close a known gap rather than leave it sitting there because it isn't proven to matter yet.

**A second, real, more serious version of the very same masking problem was found in the process.** While specifically checking whether the screens actually talk to this benchmark service, it became clear the one screen meant to show these benchmark numbers had never actually been built to talk to it at all, the specific delivery endpoint the screen calls simply did not exist yet on the main service, so that screen had been silently showing stand-in numbers this whole time regardless of the permission-check bug, a completely separate reason for the exact same symptom. This was built for real: the main service now asks the benchmark service for its latest saved report and reshapes it into the exact numbers that screen expects, rather than reading that report's file directly, so the benchmark service stays the one true source of that specific kind of number instead of two different places quietly being able to disagree about it.

**A deliberate honesty tradeoff, carried forward rather than smoothed over.** Two of the three numbers this screen shows, exact timing for the very first and very last steps of a spoken reply, do not actually exist anywhere yet, since nothing in the live voice pipeline has ever recorded them, a gap already known and written down honestly earlier in this same document. Rather than making up a number to fill the gap now that a real screen is finally looking at it, those two numbers are passed through exactly as genuinely missing, and the chart drawing them was fixed to show them plainly as not yet measured instead of quietly turning a missing number into something that looks like a real zero.

**A second, worse version of the original masking bug was found, live, while checking all of this.** A completely different screen, the parent progress view, was still quietly falling back to stand-in data even after the permission-check bug was already fixed. Digging into why turned up something worse than the first bug: this screen has always pointed at a placeholder id that was never a real, existing child, and asking the real database about a child id in a format it does not recognize was crashing that request outright, a genuine, unhandled server mistake, not a clean, expected miss. Making things worse, that specific kind of crash happened to skip past the very step that answers the permission check, so the browser could not even see that a crash had happened, it just looked, once again, exactly like the server could not be reached at all, and quietly fell back to stand-in data, hiding a real defect behind the exact same appearance as the very bug that had just been fixed. This was fixed by checking that a requested child id is even shaped like a real one before ever asking the database about it, turning what used to be a confusing crash into a plain, ordinary, correctly answered miss, exactly the same way the temporary local file used earlier in this project already handled the same situation correctly on its own.

**What this specific screen showing stand-in data now honestly means, and what was deliberately not done about it.** With that crash fixed, this same screen still shows stand-in data, but now for a plain, honest reason rather than a hidden one: the placeholder child id this screen has always used was never a real one to begin with, there is no real signed-in child yet anywhere in this project. The separate, real, permanent demo child that the live voice bot itself creates and remembers is a completely different id, and nothing yet connects the two, so a real reading session run through the voice bot still would not show up on this particular screen today. Deciding how to properly connect a screen to a specific real child, whether through an actual login system or by simply pointing the demo screens at that same real demo child, is a real product decision, not a bug fix, and was deliberately left as an open, clearly written down choice rather than being quietly decided alone.

**How every one of these was checked, for real, not assumed.** Each fix was proven against the real, currently running services, not just reasoned about. The benchmark service's own permission check was repeated by hand and confirmed to now come back allowed. The new benchmark-numbers delivery endpoint was called for real and its exact numbers were confirmed to match the benchmark service's own saved report, then confirmed a second time by actually opening that screen in a real browser and seeing a real chart with real numbers and a real "Live data" label, with the two genuinely unmeasured numbers shown plainly as not yet measured instead of a broken or blank chart. The crash fix was confirmed twice, first directly, by repeating the exact request that used to crash and watching it come back as a clean, ordinary miss instead, with the permission-check answer correctly attached this time, and then by watching the real browser's own error log stop reporting a mysterious network failure and start reporting the same plain, honest miss instead.

## Giving a finished story somewhere to go next

A real, plain gap was pointed out after all of this: once a child finished reading a story, the only thing offered was reading that exact same story again. There was no way to move on to a different one without leaving the page entirely and going back to the home screen by hand.

**What was added.** Two new options now appear alongside "Read Again" the moment a story is actually finished, not before. "Next Story" asks the exact same already-working next-story logic every other part of this project already trusts, the same one that picks a story based on what a specific child still needs practice on, for a brand new story, and swaps straight into it. "Choose a Story" simply goes to the existing progress map, for anyone who would rather pick deliberately instead of letting the system choose.

**Why this needed almost no new logic of its own.** Both of these already existed as real, working, already-tested pieces of this project, one already picks a sensible next story for any given child, and the other already lets someone tap any specific story directly. This was purely a matter of finally offering both of them at the one moment in the whole experience where a person would actually want them, right after finishing something.

**A small, deliberate design choice.** Rather than trying to carefully reset every single piece of the reading screen's own internal state by hand when a new story is chosen this way, a new story is instead treated as a genuinely new screen entirely, so it gets the exact same clean, guaranteed fresh start a full page reload already gives it, with no risk of some leftover piece of state from the previous story quietly carrying over by accident.

**How this was checked.** This was clicked through directly in a real browser, not just reasoned about. A full simulated reading session was run start to finish, and once it correctly finished, both new options appeared exactly as intended, correctly placed only after completion and not before. Clicking "Next Story" correctly cleared every part of the finished screen, the highlighted words, the finishing summary, the story text itself, and returned to a plain, ready-to-start screen exactly like a brand new visit to the page would. It happened to land on the same practice story as before only because the real skill tracker service was intentionally not running during this particular check, so the always-available stand-in logic was used instead, which deliberately always picks the same one story for the same child so a demo stays reproducible, this is a known and correct property of that stand-in, not a flaw in the new feature, and the real service's own picking logic has already been separately proven, earlier in this document, to correctly change its answer as a child's real practice history changes.

**How this was checked.** The word-picking logic itself was run directly against a handful of real stories and confirmed to correctly find a real, sensible matching word each time, and to correctly fall back to the longest word on a story where nothing matched. The full feature was then opened for real in a browser, a story was read through the existing practice mode, and the exact expected word was watched turning from a plain star into a gold, celebrated ring at exactly the right moment, live, on screen, not merely assumed to work from reading the code.

Both of these were built and checked without touching or slowing down anything already working, and both were chosen specifically because they turn real, already-trustworthy numbers into something that actually feels like a game, rather than adding a layer of decoration on top that would have undercut the honesty this project has tried to hold onto throughout.

## Making the tutor's patience visible, and an honest limit found while doing it

The mastery-aware hint pacing built earlier only ever showed up as a slightly longer pause, live, during a session, with no trace of it anywhere else. Three ways to fix that were sketched out and written down first, then the first and cheapest of them was built for real, along with the shared groundwork the other two will need later.

**What was actually built.** The moment the tutor deliberately holds a spoken hint back, the reading screen now shows a distinct, warm state, a small thinking face and the words "giving you a moment to try that one," instead of leaving the pause unexplained. This required one new, small event the voice pipeline emits the instant that decision is made, separate from the event that fires if the hint is eventually spoken. Two new numbers are also now saved with every session, how many times a hint was held back, and how many of those the child then caught on their own, so the other two ideas, a mention in the parent recap and a future engineering number, can be built later without redoing this part.

**An honest, tested limit found in the process, not assumed.** Before writing this up as fully working, an actual attempt was made to get a real, natural example of the second number, a child catching a delayed hint themselves, to happen through the real listening system, not just imagined. It did not work the way expected. The part of the system that recognizes a child catching their own mistake only ever looks at the very next word spoken, which happens well before a hint would ever be held back in the first place. A child genuinely circling back several words later to fix an earlier mistake, tested for real, was not recognized as a catch at all, it just read as two separate, unrelated mistakes. This is a real, honest limit of the current listening system, not a bug in the new counter itself, and it means that specific number will likely sit at or near zero in real use for now. Rather than write a recap sentence that would quietly overclaim credit for something that almost never actually happens, the wording was deliberately built to only ever say "we gave extra time," and only add "and they caught it" on the rare occasion that number is actually greater than zero.

**How this was checked.** Automated tests were written that prove the new event fires at exactly the right moment and in the right order, that the two new numbers count correctly when a hint is eventually spoken anyway, and, deliberately labeled as a direct test of the mechanism itself rather than a natural end to end example, that the counting logic for a genuine catch is correct even though triggering it naturally turned out to be rare. A real session was then sent through the real running service, with a real request to the AI for the recap, and the response correctly and honestly described the extra time given, in one version mentioning a successful catch and in another correctly leaving that part out entirely when it hadn't happened. Finally, the new visible state was watched appearing for real in a running browser, confirmed programmatically to appear in the right order relative to the tutor actually speaking, before the practice session data was cleared out again.

## A real readability problem, found by simply looking at the screen

While checking the work above, a good, simple observation cut through everything else being tested: the story shown on screen had no periods, commas, or question marks anywhere in it at all, just a long, unbroken stream of words. Checking this directly confirmed it immediately, the exact punctuated sentences a curator had carefully written were sitting right there in the data the whole time, but the reading screen had only ever been shown the version of the passage with every punctuation mark stripped out.

**Why that stripped version has to exist, and why it was still a real problem.** The reason that stripped-down list of words exists at all is a genuine, necessary one, it is the exact list the live listening system compares against what a child actually says out loud, and spoken language does not come with commas attached. That part was never the mistake. The mistake was letting the screen a child actually reads from use that same stripped list directly, when a young reader genuinely depends on punctuation to know where one sentence ends and where to naturally pause.

**The fix, and why it could not just add punctuation back carelessly.** A small, separate piece of logic was written that lines up the original, fully punctuated version of a story against the plain word list already used everywhere else, matching them up word by word, and pulls just the punctuation marks back out to show on screen, without ever touching or changing the underlying plain word list the listening system depends on. If any single word ever failed to line up cleanly between the two versions, that one word is deliberately left unpunctuated rather than risk placing a punctuation mark in the wrong spot.

**How this was checked.** The matching logic was run directly against a real, full story from the library, including a tricky part with quoted dialogue and a contraction, and the punctuated sentence it rebuilt from the two separate pieces matched the original, correctly written story exactly, character for character. The actual reading screen was then reloaded in a real browser and visually confirmed to now show full, correct punctuation throughout the same story that had been missing it entirely just before.

## The two halves of this project were never actually connected, until now

While asking how to actually run and try the finished product, a simple, honest question cut straight through weeks of separately-verified work: why did the on-screen story keep reading itself out loud on its own, even with total silence in the room? The direct answer was uncomfortable but important to say plainly: the polished screen a child would actually look at, and the real system that actually listens with real ears, had never once been connected to each other. Both halves were genuinely real and had each been proven working entirely on their own, but nothing had ever wired them together into one single thing a real child could actually sit down and use.

**Why this had quietly happened.** Building a nice-looking screen and building a real listening system are two very different kinds of work, and they were sensibly built in parallel by treating each other as a placeholder at first, exactly like most software gets built. The screen was built and proven against a small script that simply walks through a story's words on a timer, standing in for a real conversation that didn't exist yet. The real listening system was separately built and proven with an actual human voice and real ears, but was only ever tested through its own bare, generic testing page, with none of the child-facing polish. The step that was supposed to come last, replacing the screen's stand-in script with the real thing, never actually happened, and nothing forced that gap to become visible until someone actually tried using the finished-looking product as a whole.

**A second, deeper problem hiding underneath the first.** Digging into how to actually connect these two pieces for real surfaced a second, completely separate, previously unknown bug sitting inside the real listening system itself, one that had been there through every previous successful voice test without ever being caught. The part of that system responsible for sending its live, moment to moment updates, such as which word was just heard, or that a hint is being spoken, was accidentally built to only work with one specific way of connecting to it, while the actual way it had always been run and tested this whole time was a different, more independent method that ignored that message entirely. In plain terms, every single earlier voice test genuinely proved the system could hear and speak correctly, but had never actually proven it could report back live what it was hearing while doing so, because the one channel meant to carry that information had quietly never worked at all, the entire time.

**How the real, correct fix was found, not guessed at.** Rather than patching this by trial and error, the actual underlying real time communication system this project depends on was read directly and carefully to find the one, official, correctly designed way to send this kind of live update regardless of which specific connection method happens to be running underneath. A small, one-line change was made to send updates that way instead. Making sure this was actually the right fix, and not just a plausible-sounding one, took real, hands-on proof: a tiny, separate, throwaway test page was built from scratch specifically to watch for these updates arriving, the real listening system was started for real, and a single temporary test message was sent through the newly fixed pathway. It arrived, correctly and completely, confirming the entire fix before anything else was built on top of it. That temporary test message was then removed immediately afterward, its job done.

**Connecting the two real halves together for the first time.** With that confirmed working, the screen was finally given a real way to talk to the real listening system directly, replacing the old stand-in timer script for good, while keeping that same old script available on purpose as an honest, clearly labeled fallback rather than deleting it. If the real listening system cannot be reached for any reason, the screen says so plainly and drops back to the simulated version instead of silently pretending nothing is wrong, matching the same honesty this project has tried to hold onto in every other part of it.

**How this was proven to work, for real, end to end.** Both directions were tested for real, not assumed. With the real listening system deliberately turned off, starting a story on the real screen correctly showed a clear message explaining it could not connect, before smoothly continuing on with the simulated version exactly as designed. With the real listening system turned back on, the exact same screen instead correctly showed a real, live connected confirmation, and the real system's own internal logs, checked directly and independently rather than just trusted, showed it had genuinely generated and begun speaking its real opening greeting out loud in response, with real, measured timing, exactly as it would for an actual child sitting down to use it for the first time.

**What this still does not yet prove, said plainly.** The specific tool used to check all of this automatically is not able to turn on a real microphone the way an actual person can, so this pass proves the connection itself, and the live reporting channel riding on top of it, are both now genuinely real and working, but it does not yet prove what happens once a real child actually speaks into it through this newly connected path. That is now honestly the very next thing left to try, with a real voice, before anything else.

### The very first real attempt at using it found a second real bug, immediately

Almost the moment a real person actually tried the newly connected system with a real voice, something was clearly wrong: the wrong story appeared to be shown, and the tutor seemed to interrupt constantly, far more than intended, treating almost everything said as a mistake.

**The real cause, found fast because the two symptoms pointed to the same thing.** The on-screen story and the real listening system had genuinely never been told to agree on which single story they were both supposed to be using at the same time. The screen was still independently asking for its own story using an old placeholder identity that was never actually connected to any real progress record, so it always quietly picked one story from a small local backup list on its own. The real listening system, completely separately, picked a real, different story on its own too. A person would see one story on their screen while the system silently compared every word they said against a completely different one, which explains both problems as the exact same root cause: nearly everything said would look like a mistake, because it was genuinely being checked against the wrong text entirely, which is also exactly why the coach seemed to interrupt so constantly.

**The fix.** The real listening system now clearly announces, the moment it starts, exactly which story it actually picked, and the screen was changed to treat that announcement as the one correct answer the instant it arrives, replacing whatever it may have already guessed on its own beforehand.

**How this was checked.** The real listening system was started for real again, and the exact story it spoke out loud in its own greeting was compared directly against what the screen showed after connecting. Both matched exactly this time, "Pat and the Big Hat" in both places, confirmed independently from the system's own internal log and from the screen itself, not just assumed fixed because the code looked right.

**Two more honest things noticed while checking this, neither hidden.** First, during this exact check, the separate service responsible for real per-student progress and story selection happened not to be running, so the real listening system correctly and safely fell back to a single default practice story instead, exactly as it was always designed to when that service is unreachable. This was not a new problem, it is a documented, intentional safety fallback, but it is also a likely second real contributor to the earlier complaint about constant interruptions: without a real, saved progress record to check against, every single skill looks completely unpracticed, and the system is deliberately designed to help quickly and immediately on anything unpracticed, rather than waiting, exactly as intended, just more noticeably so with a completely fresh, empty progress record. Second, an unrelated, harmless warning was noticed in the real listening system's own log about an optional, separate piece of the underlying framework not being set up. Checking into it directly confirmed it only affects a handful of extra, automatic convenience messages this project was never using or relying on in the first place, so it does not affect anything already checked and working, and was left alone rather than chased down for its own sake.

### One command instead of three, and a small buffering bug caught before it shipped

Having to start three separate things by hand every time, in three separate terminal windows, was raised as a fair, real complaint, worth fixing rather than explaining away. A single script was added at the very top of the project that starts all three real services together with one command, and stops all three together with a single Ctrl+C, rather than merging them into one process, since they remain genuinely different kinds of things that should stay separate.

The very first version of this script tried to also neatly label each service's output with its own name, by piping each one's real output through a small text processing step first. Testing that version directly, rather than trusting that it looked correct, showed a real, meaningful problem: that extra step quietly held onto output instead of showing it right away, sometimes not showing anything at all until a service was already being shut down. For a script whose entire purpose is letting a person see a real startup problem the moment it happens, silently delayed or lost output would have been worse than no script at all. That labeling step was removed entirely in favor of a plainer version that simply lets each service's own real output appear immediately, unlabeled, since each one already clearly announces its own name in its own normal output anyway.

## The first real read-aloud session, and an honest split between a real fix and two known limits

The first time this was actually used the way it is meant to be used, reading a real story out loud to it, three things looked wrong at once: a couple of words got a plain visual mark with no spoken explanation, the very last question at the end seemed to just get asked and then nothing happened afterward, and pressing the button to try again brought up a new story but nothing about it actually worked.

Each of these was treated separately, honestly, and only two of the three could actually be checked directly.

**Trying it again did not work, and this one was checked as thoroughly as currently possible.** A separate, deliberately isolated copy of the real listening system was started purely for this check, so the version already in real use would not be disturbed. A real connection was made, then deliberately disconnected, then reconnected a second time, exactly mimicking what pressing the button again actually does underneath. The second connection worked completely correctly on its own, correctly starting a whole fresh session and correctly announcing a fresh story again, which rules out the real listening system itself being left in some broken, unusable state after a first use. While checking this closely, though, a real, separate, legitimate timing problem was found and fixed anyway: the screen was starting a brand new connection, including asking to use the microphone again, before it had actually finished properly closing down the previous one, a real race between shutting one down and starting the next that is now closed by making the screen properly wait for the old one to fully finish first. Said plainly and honestly: this specific check could not use an actual microphone, since the tool available for testing here is not able to grant that, so this proves the underlying reconnection mechanism itself is sound and closes one real, legitimate risk, but it does not yet fully prove or rule out everything that could happen with real audio specifically across two back to back real sessions.

**The other two were, on close reading, already known and named limitations, not new mistakes, though this could not be checked as directly.** The plain visual mark with no spoken follow-up matches a deliberate, already-existing design choice made earlier in this project on purpose, spoken help is intentionally not given for absolutely every single flagged word, both to avoid talking over a child constantly and because a skill already going well is deliberately given a moment first rather than interrupted immediately. The abrupt stop right after the very last question also matches a specific, already written down, already accepted limitation from earlier in this project: real turn-taking, correctly knowing exactly when a person has truly finished speaking versus just paused, was explicitly built as a simplified stand-in for now rather than a fully solved problem, and was already flagged in writing as something to properly revisit later rather than something quietly assumed to be perfect. Both of these are genuinely plausible, well-documented explanations rather than a shrug, but neither could be confirmed with full certainty the way the reconnection issue was, since doing so would need the real system's own detailed internal log from that exact real session, which was not available after the fact to check directly.

## Letting a real person actually choose which story to read

Up to this point, the story a child reads was always picked automatically, with no way for anyone to ask for a specific one instead, a real limitation for actually showing off different parts of this project side by side rather than waiting on an automatic pick to eventually land on the right example.

The screen showing why a particular story was picked now also correctly and honestly says so when a story was picked this way, plainly, rather than reusing language written for the automatic picking rule.

**A real, official mechanism was tried first, and honestly found not to work.** The underlying real time voice framework this project depends on has its own official, documented way for a screen to hand a small piece of custom information, like which story was picked, directly along with the same request that starts a live listening session. This was proven directly rather than assumed, by adding a temporary line of logging to the real listening system and watching it directly, in real time, as a real connection came in: the custom information the screen sent, using the framework's own official method, correctly documented, simply never arrived on the listening side at all, arriving as nothing every single time. **How this was proven to work, for real, more than once.** A real, temporary, separate copy of the real listening system was used for this entire check, specifically so the version already being used for real testing was never touched or disturbed. ## A real recording, watched frame by frame, and three real fixes from it

A real screen recording of someone actually using the app, start to finish, was gone through carefully rather than skimmed, and it surfaced several real problems no amount of reading the code alone would have caught. Three of them were fixed right away.

**A button that claimed a story had already been read once, when it hadn't been.** Right at the very start of a brand new story, before a single word had been read out loud, the button already said "Read Again" instead of "Start Reading." The actual cause was simple once found: the flag controlling that label was being set the instant the button was first pressed at all, not once a read had genuinely finished, so it flipped early and stayed wrong for the entire first read. A second, separate flag was added specifically to track whether a read has ever actually finished, left alone by every later press so it only changes at the one moment that should change it, and the button now correctly waits to say "Read Again" until there is genuinely something to read again.

**A coaching tip that outlived the session it was about.** In the recording, a real spoken hint bubble, "Try again with that sound," was still sitting on screen at the exact same moment the "Great reading!" finishing summary, the score, and the Read Again button were all already showing, two contradictory messages on screen together. The piece of code that marks a session finished never told the hint to go away, so if a hint happened to still be mid-display right when the story ended, it simply kept showing until its own unrelated timer ran out on its own schedule, regardless of what else had already happened. The fix clears it the instant the session is marked finished, so a leftover coaching tip can no longer contradict the "you're done" screen it now shares no time with.

**Two different, unconnected "demo children."** This is the more serious one. This whole project has, this entire time, actually had two completely separate pretend children living in two separate places: one the on-screen parts have always pointed at, a placeholder identifier that was never a real, existing child in the real database, and a second, real, different one that the live listening system creates and remembers entirely on its own, in its own separate file, with no connection whatsoever between the two. This meant a real, live reading session, actually spoken out loud through an actual microphone, could be completed successfully from beginning to end, and the parent progress screen would still show nothing real about it, forever, no matter how many real sessions ever happened, simply because the two sides were quietly talking about two different children the whole time. The recording made this unmistakable: real sessions were completed on screen, and the progress screen right afterward still plainly said "Mock data." The fix creates exactly one single, shared, well-known child identity that both sides now ask for by name, whichever side asks first creates it for real, and every side afterward, forever after, gets back that exact same one. Both sides were updated to ask for this same one child instead of each quietly inventing their own.

**A second, genuinely separate problem, found only because the first fix finally let it surface.** With both sides finally pointed at the same real child, the progress map screen still, oddly, kept falling back to stand-in data even though the real child genuinely now existed with real data behind it. Digging into why revealed a real, previously invisible slowness problem: figuring out how strong a child is at each of the eighteen tracked skills was written as eighteen separate individual requests to the real, now-online database, one per skill, one at a time, back to back, instead of one single request asking for all eighteen at once. Because the real database lives on a different machine out on the internet now, not on this laptop, every one of those individual requests carried real network travel time, and eighteen of them stacked up back to back took close to two full seconds, long enough that the screen's own safety timer, built to give up and quietly fall back to stand-in data if a real answer takes too long, kept giving up right before the real answer actually arrived. This exact kind of problem had already been solved correctly once before, for a different, similar screen elsewhere in this same file, asking for all eighteen skill levels in one single combined request instead of eighteen separate ones. The exact same already-proven fix was applied here too. This dropped that screen's real answer time from just under two full seconds to well under one second, comfortably inside the safety timer's limit.

**How every one of these three was actually proven, not just assumed fixed.** The button wording fix was watched directly mid-read, confirmed still correctly saying "Start Reading" a second and a half into a brand new story, with the button also correctly still disabled at that exact moment. The shared-child fix was proven by directly asking the real database, twice, back to back, for that same one shared identity, and getting back the exact same one both times, then completing an entire real practice session for real under that identity and watching the parent progress screen immediately reflect those exact real numbers with a real "Live data" label instead of "Mock data" for the first time ever. The slowness fix was proven with a real stopwatch, timing the exact same real request before and after, confirming the actual measured improvement rather than assuming the smaller number of requests must obviously be faster. The full, already-existing set of automated checks for this whole part of the project was also run again afterward, on a separate, deliberately isolated copy so the real, already-live data already in real use was never touched by testing, and every single one still passed. Afterward, the one fabricated test result created purely to prove the shared-child fix was itself deliberately deleted from the real database, specifically so nothing invented for testing purposes would ever be mistaken later for a real child's real reading progress.

## The last two things from the recording

**The same slowness bug, found a second time, in a different place, with worse consequences.** The exact same "eighteen separate requests instead of one" mistake fixed above for the progress map turned out to also exist in the piece of logic that picks a child's very next story, a completely different, unrelated part of the code that happened to make the identical mistake independently. Here the consequence was worse than a slow screen: the real listening system asks for a story with a strict two second patience limit before giving up and quietly using a plain backup story instead, one that was never actually written to include the extra information used to explain why a story was picked or to mark a special challenge word inside it. The real request was taking just under two full seconds on its own, right at the edge of that patience limit, so it would frequently, unpredictably lose that race, and the moment it did, the "why this story was picked" sentence and the challenge word star, both real and both genuinely present a moment earlier from the screen's own separate loading of the same story, would quietly vanish once the listening system's own, less complete version of the story arrived and replaced it. The same fix already proven for the progress map, one combined request instead of eighteen separate ones, was applied here too, brought the real answer time down to under one second, and confirmed directly, twice, that the extra information now consistently arrives intact instead of disappearing.

**A real mistake, found by deliberately trying to prove it was already fine.** The recording showed several words right at the very start of a story getting quietly marked wrong even though the story had, by every visible sign, been read correctly. Rather than guess, this was tested directly and honestly, completely separately from any live audio: the exact real story from the recording was fed through the exact real word-by-word checking logic, word for word, in perfect order, with nothing said wrong at all, and it still falsely reported the very first word as skipped entirely. The cause turned out to be a real, previously known but seriously underestimated flaw in how that checking logic works moment to moment: while a story is still mostly unread, it compares whatever has been said so far against the entire rest of the story still to come, and when a word said early on happens to repeat again later in the story, which is extremely common in simple stories written for young children, for example a character's name used again in a later sentence, the checking logic can end up confused about which occurrence of that word is genuinely the one currently being read, and can wrongly decide the earlier one must have been skipped. This had already been written down honestly, once before, as a known, accepted, supposedly rare limitation. It turned out not to be rare at all for the exact kind of simple, repetitive writing this whole project is built around, and could genuinely strike on the very first word of a story, exactly matching what the recording showed. The fix does not try to outsmart the underlying confusion directly. Instead, while a story is still being read live, the checking logic is now only ever allowed to compare against a short, nearby stretch of the story just ahead of where the child actually is, never the entire remaining story at once, which removes the confusing situation from ever arising in the first place.
